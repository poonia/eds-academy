Block catalog governance

Without a gatekeeper, three teams build three carousels, and none of them is accessible.

Keep a catalog of blocks with owner, purpose, variants, content model, accessibility status and example page. A new block should meet a bar: is it needed by several pages, and can an existing block or default content do the job?

Reuse Adobe’s Block Collection as a starting point, but adapt deliberately. With repoless, a code change reaches all sites, so catalog changes need a wider review. Retire unused blocks: find usage in the index and telemetry, migrate the content, then remove the code.

Authoring governance

Fast publishing is the benefit of EDS. Governance decides who can use that speed, and on what.

Define who can preview, publish and change sheets such as redirects, metadata and nav. These have site-wide effects. Provide templates, a style guide and required metadata. Use roles to separate authors from publishers.

For regulated content, combine the tools you have: snapshot reviews, Request Publish, where onboarded, or AEM workflows. Confirm that the audit trail meets your requirements. The audit log records operations, but your compliance team must decide if it is enough.

Accessibility compliance: WCAG 2.2 AA, AODA

Accessibility is a legal duty in many places, and an ongoing operating practice rather than a launch task.

In Ontario, the AODA’s Integrated Accessibility Standards require designated public sector organizations, and organizations with 50 or more employees, to meet WCAG 2.0 Level AA on public websites and on web content posted after 1 January 2012. Live captions (1.2.4) and pre-recorded audio descriptions (1.2.5) are excepted. Ontario’s guidance says internal intranets and extranets need not meet those levels.

WCAG 2.2 AA is the sensible target, because it includes WCAG 2.0 AA’s requirements, apart from the removed 4.1.1 Parsing, and adds criteria such as target size and focus not obscured. Treat this as technical guidance, and have counsel confirm your duties.

Build it into the operation: accessible blocks, author training, automated checks in pull requests, manual keyboard and screen reader testing, and a route for people to report barriers.

Code standards

Shared rules let people move between projects and reuse code from Adobe’s examples.

The boilerplate runs eslint with airbnb-base and stylelint. Adobe advises against changing the lint rules, since it makes it harder to reuse boilerplate and Block Collection code. Personal preference is not a good reason.

Protect main, require approval, and include test URLs in each pull request. A pull request should have a green Lighthouse score, ideally 100 on mobile and desktop. Keep scope small. With coding agents, talk to maintainers before opening a large change.

RACI and support model

An incident at 2 a.m. is the wrong time to learn that no one owns the CDN.

Use RACI to make ownership explicit: one person is Accountable, others are Responsible, Consulted or Informed. Cover content, blocks, configuration, CDN, security, analytics and accessibility.

Define support tiers. L1 handles author how-to questions. L2 handles site issues in your team. L3 is Adobe, through your support route and collaboration channel. Agree on severity levels, response targets and who talks to the business. Adobe’s SLOs do not replace these.

Hands-on lab: write a one-page operating model

Goal

Make ownership and standards explicit.

You need

A real or sample site, and two colleagues.

Time

About 90 minutes

  1. List the top 10 recurring decisions.
  2. Complete a RACI for them.
  3. Rank content by risk.
  4. Choose a control for each.
  5. Set up a block catalog with three blocks.
  6. Draft the accessibility routine.
  7. Write support tiers and severity levels.

Expected result

A one-page operating model.

How to verify

Each decision has one Accountable person, and your colleagues agree it matches reality.

Stretch

Test it with a mock incident.

  • One owner per decision
  • Controls tiered
  • Catalog started
  • Support tiers written
